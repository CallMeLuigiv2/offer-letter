The rule

You earn code. Nobody hands it to you.

Hiring in 2026 tests exactly this. New-grad postings at Vercel, Stripe and Pinterest now say to use AI but verify every line, and interview loops make you read, debug and review code you didn't write. So the core loop of Offer Letter is a five-step gate that a teammate's code must pass through before it becomes yours.

  1. Review the PRA teammate's pull request with bugs planted on purpose. The harness holds the answer key and scores what you catch and what you flag wrongly.
  2. Take the quizClick questions about this exact code. Answers are checked by running it: predict the output, name the test that breaks if line 42 changes.
  3. Explain itA voice call with the author. You walk them through it; they ask the questions a reviewer would.
  4. Change itThe PM asks for a small change. You make it with no help, and the tests decide.
  5. Own itThe repo is pushed to your GitHub. Commits keep their real authors, so the history shows the teammate wrote v1 and you own it now.
TICKET
KLN-01 · Fix the load generator before we trust its p99
COMPANY
Kilnworks, an LLM inference API. Level L1, about 5 hours.
WHY
Load generators that wait for each response before sending the next one hide the very stalls you're trying to measure. The research found measurement bugs like this make the best planted bugs, because they look like working code.
DONE
Your review catches the planted issues, the fixed generator reproduces a stall the old one hid, and the quiz is passed.

Paper tickets

38 of the 139 tickets come from research papers or engineering blogs, with 50 more ideas in the bank. Framed as "our team wants to try this paper in production", they come in three forms:

  • Reproduce: get the paper's main effect on a small setup, as a ratio against a baseline run on the same machine. Unless a ticket says otherwise, you pass at 80% of the paper's gain, or 50% when the result depends on hardware scale. You also list where your setup differs from the paper's and run an ablation showing the gain mostly disappears without the key piece.
  • Apply: make the technique work in the company's code, behind a flag.
  • Adopt memo: one page on "should we?". The system description hides one fact that breaks the paper's key assumption, and you pass only if you name it.

Ideas from other fields

About a third of the paper ideas come from outside computing. Each one has a real precedent:

  • Airline seat pricing → selling blocks of GPU time.
  • Factory quality control → spotting drift in model inputs.
  • Psychology test design → shrinking an eval suite to a few questions.
  • Epidemic models → gossip protocols for failure detection.
  • Honey-bee foraging → sharing servers between services.

Your team

Six coworkers who disagree with you for real reasons

AI assistants tend to agree with whoever they're talking to, which would make the team useless. So every teammate follows one rule: they change their mind for evidence or a better alternative, never because you repeated yourself. About 1 in 4 of their objections is planted wrong, and catching those is part of the job. Try both kinds below.

What makes them feel like people

  • A character sheet, not a quirk: this quarter's goal, what worries them, a blind spot, how they write, when they're out.
  • Memory of what you did and promised, plus plans of their own. A 2023 study of AI characters found that without memory and plans they were the least believable.
  • The sheet is re-sent every turn, because AI characters drift from their instructions within about 8 exchanges.

What keeps them honest

  • Everyone is competent. Friction comes from interests you can discover by asking, never from villainy.
  • They only know what their role would know: the PM hasn't read the code, the junior missed the incident.
  • Replies take realistic time, and people are sometimes out of office.

Your week

A job that fits around your real one

Firm but fair: deadlines are real, but they only count your office days. A miss comes up once in standup and once in your review, and nobody nags. You can book days off ahead of time.

Clocking in

Tap your badge and tell the app how long you have: 30, 60, 90 minutes, or open-ended. Tickets come in 30–90 minute work blocks, so even a short session finishes something. The team keeps working overnight, so there are new commits and messages waiting in the morning.

When the plan runs low

Teammates run on your Claude plan. Past 70% of the 5-hour window they switch to the smallest model and pause background work. Past 90%, the team is "out to lunch until 3:40" and you get offline work: reading, quizzes graded by code, benchmark runs.

The placement interview

  • A. Codebase dive (60 min): read a 5,000-line service, fix a planted bug without AI, then add a feature with it.
  • B. PR review (40 min): six planted issues, including one made-up API call of the kind AI writes.
  • C. Measure and fix (45 min): name the hotspot before profiling, then fix to a target.
  • D. Design-lite (45 min): for example, a rate-limited gateway in front of an LLM API.
  • E. Fundamentals (15 min): click quizzes.
  • F. Paper read (20 min, optional).

You start at the lowest level that passes A and B, the two skills hardest to fake with AI, and never above L3. A and B rerun every six weeks with fresh code, so your level always rests on recent evidence.

Feedback

No points. Debriefs and real consequences instead.

You want it to feel like a game, and it will, through the world, the characters and the story. The research is clear about scoring systems, though. In a classroom study, badges and leaderboards lowered motivation and exam scores. Rewards you expect to get undermine the drive to learn. And feedback hurt performance in about a third of studied cases, mostly when it was aimed at the person rather than the work. Feedback on the work itself is the single most important feature of simulation-based learning.

After every piece of work

A short debrief about the work itself: what the review caught, what it missed, which number moved, and what a senior would have done differently.

Progress you can see

Promotions with real scope: on-call duty, design-review lead, the junior to mentor. Your desk shows things you actually shipped, like a framed link to a merged PR.

What stays

Your ID badge is who you are at the company, not a reward. The office changes because of what happened, not because you hit a score.

Teammates on your plan

  • Each teammate is the regular claude program in headless mode, signed in with your own login. Anthropic's docs explicitly allow signing in to the unmodified program with your own plan; the Agent SDK page points developers to API keys, so the SDK isn't used.
  • Never the --bare flag: it skips your login, and the docs say it will become the default later. The app checks your sign-in at startup and refuses to run on an API key.
  • At most two Claude processes at a time. Haiku for chat and standups, Sonnet for writing and reviewing PRs, Opus for design reviews and the senior engineer.
  • Heavy content (planted-bug PRs, decision notes, onboarding docs) is generated once per company ahead of time, not live.

Faces and voices

  • Portraits: one approved image per character, seven expressions made by repainting only the face, three mouth and two eye overlays, and mouth movement driven by the audio's loudness.
  • Generated locally on your RTX 4070 with ComfyUI and Animagine XL 4.0, about 20 minutes of GPU time per character.
  • Voices: Kokoro has about 16 good English voices, only 5 male, so male teammates use blended voices. A blind test checks they sound like different people.
  • Audio stays in Python, reusing grokbot's pipeline.

Decisions for you

Six calls before the build starts

  1. Rewards

    In-world consequences and debriefs only, or add some game-style rewards anyway?

    Recommended: in-world only, as above. The research against scores is strong.

  2. WSL update test

    Run wsl --update and restart WSL, then re-check KVM and cgroup v2. It could unblock two Belljar tickets, but it changes your WSL.

    Recommended: yes, when you're not mid-project in WSL.

  3. Office art

    Buy LimeZu's Modern Interiors and Modern Office packs on itch.io (kept on your PC only), or start with the free Kenney packs.

    Recommended: LimeZu. It's the look in the mockup, and Kenney stays as the public fallback.

  4. Portrait setup

    Install ComfyUI and Animagine XL 4.0 (several GB), and spend about 20 minutes of GPU time per character.

    Recommended: yes, at v0.2. v0.1 is text-only.

  5. 30 minutes of testing

    A blind voice test (about 20 minutes) and a push-to-talk test in VS Code (about 10 minutes).

    Recommended: when v0.2 starts.

  6. Your first company

    You choose on the careers page during onboarding. The app can lock the choice to growth-stage companies for your first job, or show all 15.

    Recommended: growth-stage first, everything unlocked from your second job.

Where it goes

Built to grow

More domains

Games and engines, security, data and analytics. A company is a pack: team, codebase, backlog and culture, so adding one doesn't touch the app.

Changing jobs

After a few months, leave. The placement interview becomes a real interview loop at the next company, with a new stack and a new culture.

Real open-source issues

Over 40 real repos with live starter issues are already listed. Teammates help you scope one; the upstream PR is 100% yours.

A brag doc that writes itself

Every shipped ticket adds a line with its numbers to a résumé draft you can edit.

New papers, new tickets

The paper bank grows as papers come out. Each one becomes a reproduce, apply or adopt-memo ticket.

Real people

Later, friends could join as teammates, and messages could reach your phone through the local server.
